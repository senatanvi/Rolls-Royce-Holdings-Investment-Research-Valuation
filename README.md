# Rolls-Royce Holdings plc — Equity Research Project

> **Student investment research | LSE: RR. | Data in this draft primarily through 11 September 2026**

## Research question

**Has Rolls-Royce's transformation created enough value to justify the company's current market valuation?**

## Preliminary investment view

**Preliminary view: BUY — hypothesis to be tested**

This is a student research project rather than investment advice. The initial BUY view is a starting hypothesis, not a conclusion. The purpose of the analysis is to test whether Rolls-Royce's operational improvement, cash-flow generation and medium-term growth prospects are sufficient to justify the valuation implied by the share price.

The analysis focuses on:

- Civil Aerospace and the importance of engine flying hours
- Long-term service agreements and aftermarket economics
- Defence and Power Systems growth
- Historical margin and free-cash-flow improvement
- Forecast operating performance
- DCF valuation and valuation sensitivity
- Key risks that could invalidate the thesis

---

## 1. Investment thesis

### Why the transformation matters

Rolls-Royce has undergone a significant operational and financial transformation since the pandemic. The core question for an investor is no longer simply whether the business has recovered, but whether the improvement in margins and cash generation can be sustained.

The bull case rests on several factors:

1. **Civil Aerospace recovery** — higher widebody engine flying hours support recurring aftermarket revenue.
2. **Aftermarket economics** — long-term service agreements create recurring revenue over the life of the installed engine base.
3. **Margin expansion** — operational and commercial changes have materially improved profitability.
4. **Cash generation** — stronger free cash flow has supported debt reduction and shareholder returns.
5. **Defence and Power Systems** — these businesses provide additional sources of growth beyond civil aviation.

### What would make the thesis wrong?

The thesis is sensitive to:

- weaker-than-expected engine flying hours;
- aerospace supply-chain constraints;
- margin compression;
- slower growth in Power Systems;
- weaker defence demand;
- excessive capital allocation;
- a higher cost of capital;
- a valuation that already discounts most of the expected improvement.

# 2. Business overview

## Civil Aerospace

Rolls-Royce has a strong position in large engines used on widebody aircraft. The economics of this division extend beyond the initial sale of an engine.

The aftermarket is particularly important. Long-term service agreements such as TotalCare link revenue to engine utilisation and maintenance over the engine's operating life.

This creates an important investment relationship:

**More aircraft flying → more engine flying hours → greater aftermarket activity → higher recurring revenue and cash generation.**

The durability of this model is therefore a key assumption in the investment thesis.

## Defence

The Defence division serves military customers across areas including combat aircraft, transport and naval propulsion.

The investment case depends partly on sustained defence spending and the company's position on long-term programmes.

## Power Systems

Power Systems provides another source of diversification. Its exposure includes power-generation applications, including demand associated with data-centre infrastructure.

# 3. Sector drivers

The main industry drivers considered in this research are:

### Civil aviation recovery
Higher long-haul utilisation increases engine flying hours and supports aftermarket activity.

### Defence spending
Higher defence budgets can support demand across military aviation and naval programmes.

### Data-centre infrastructure
Growth in data-centre capacity can support demand for power-generation equipment.

### Small modular reactors
Rolls-Royce SMR represents a longer-term potential growth opportunity, although its contribution to near-term valuation should be treated cautiously.

### Sustainable aviation
Developments in sustainable aviation fuels and related technologies may influence the long-term aerospace market.

That distinction is central to determining whether the current valuation is justified.

# 6. DCF valuation

## Valuation framework

The DCF estimates the present value of future unlevered free cash flow.

**Enterprise Value = PV of forecast FCF + PV of terminal value**

Then:

**Equity Value = Enterprise Value − Net Debt**

A net cash position is treated as negative net debt, meaning cash is added when moving from enterprise value to equity value.

### Original draft valuation

The original analysis used:

- Market-implied enterprise value: approximately £117.85bn
- Net cash: approximately £2.14bn
- Shares outstanding: approximately 8.32bn
- Market price used: approximately 1,430p
- External/base-case DCF estimate: approximately 793p per share

These figures should be treated as a **starting point for the model**, not as final results. The next version of the project should replace externally generated DCF outputs with a fully transparent model built from the project's own assumptions.

# 7. Sensitivity analysis

The valuation is particularly sensitive to:

### Revenue growth
Lower growth reduces future free cash flow and terminal value.

### Operating margin
Because Rolls-Royce has significant operating leverage, a sustained margin difference can materially affect valuation.

### WACC
A higher discount rate reduces the present value of long-duration cash flows.

### Terminal growth
Terminal growth has a significant impact on DCF valuation and should be tested across a reasonable range.

A professional model should include a **WACC × terminal-growth sensitivity table** and, ideally, additional sensitivities for revenue growth and operating margin.

# 8. Key risks

## 1. Aerospace supply chain

Production constraints or supplier disruption could limit growth and delay cash generation.

## 2. Engine flying hours

The Civil Aerospace thesis depends heavily on continued high utilisation of the installed engine base.

## 3. Margin normalisation

Some recent margin improvement may reflect unusually favourable conditions or contract effects that cannot be repeated indefinitely.

## 4. Valuation risk

Even if the business performs well, investors can experience poor returns if the market valuation already discounts the expected improvement.

## 5. Capital allocation

Large buybacks and other capital-allocation decisions should be assessed against the company's underlying cash-generation capacity.

# 9. What would change my view?

The purpose of an investment thesis is not to defend a BUY rating. It is to define the evidence that would cause the thesis to change.

### Evidence supporting the thesis

- sustained engine flying-hour growth;
- continued free-cash-flow conversion;
- durable operating-margin improvement;
- successful execution of medium-term targets;
- strong Defence and Power Systems performance;
- valuation becoming more attractive relative to intrinsic value.

### Evidence weakening the thesis

- deterioration in engine utilisation;
- persistent supply-chain disruption;
- falling margins;
- materially lower free cash flow;
- weaker execution against medium-term targets;
- a valuation that becomes increasingly dependent on optimistic terminal assumptions.

# 10. Conclusion

Rolls-Royce presents an interesting case study because the investment question is not simply whether the company has improved.

The more important question is:

> **How much of the improvement is already reflected in the share price, and how much further improvement is required to justify the valuation?**

The next stage of this project is therefore to build an independent three-statement forecast and DCF model, test multiple scenarios, and compare the implied valuation with relevant aerospace and engineering peers.
